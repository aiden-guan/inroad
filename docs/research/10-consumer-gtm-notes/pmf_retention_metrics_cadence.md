## 1. PMF signals for consumer: Sean Ellis 40% test, the Superhuman PMF engine, flattening retention curves, Lenny's retention benchmarks by category, D1/D7/D30, smile curves

### Takeaway
The two encodable PMF tests are both well documented. The first is a survey signal: at least 40% of qualified users say "very disappointed", measured on the segment you are building for. The second is a behavioral signal: cohort retention curves flatten instead of decaying to zero, and they flatten at or above category benchmarks. For 6-month user retention, Lenny Rachitsky and Casey Winters put "good" and "great" at 25%/45% for consumer social, 30%/50% for consumer transactional and 40%/70% for consumer SaaS. The 2015 Andrew Chen/Quettra mobile data still frames D1 as the make-or-break period. A "smile" (cohort curve bending back up) is a strong but rarer signal, and it shows up mostly in revenue or GMV cohorts and in resurrection-heavy products.

### Cited Findings
**Sean Ellis "very disappointed" test**
- The core question is "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" with answers A) Very disappointed, B) Somewhat disappointed, C) Not disappointed. Source: [First Round Review, Rahul Vohra, "How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product/Market Fit"](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/), reposted on the [Superhuman blog](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/).
- The 40% threshold comes from Ellis's benchmarking across startups. Per the extract, Vohra relays that "Companies that struggled to find growth almost always had less than 40%" "very disappointed", while companies with strong traction usually exceeded it. Source: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/). A secondary write-up stresses it is "a rule of thumb from Ellis's experience across many startups, not a law": [IdeaPlan](https://www.ideaplan.io/case-studies/superhuman-product-market-fit).
- Who to survey: Ellis's eligibility rule is people who have experienced the core of the product at least twice and used it within the last two weeks. Superhuman's first survey went to users who had "used Superhuman at least twice in the last two weeks". Sources: [Founder Labs summary](https://writings.founderlabs.io/p/how-superhuman-went-from-22-to-58) and [pshimanshu summary](https://pshimanshu.substack.com/p/heres-what-i-learned-about-product), both secondary summaries of Vohra.
- Benchmark data point: in 2015 Hiten Shah asked Ellis's question to 731 Slack users in an open research project, and 51% said they would be very disappointed without Slack. Source: [First Round Review (Vohra)](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/), per extract.
- Ellis himself says a universal, metric-based definition of fit "has proven elusive" and recommends studying your "must-have" users. Source: [SaaSquatch recap of Sean Ellis on the stages of growth](https://www.saasquatch.com/blog/sean-ellis-recap-on-the-stages-of-growth/).

**Superhuman PMF engine (Rahul Vohra)**
- The four survey questions: (1) "How would you feel if you could no longer use Superhuman?" (very / somewhat / not disappointed); (2) "What type of people do you think would most benefit from Superhuman?"; (3) "What is the main benefit you receive from Superhuman?"; (4) "How can we improve Superhuman for you?" Sources: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Superhuman blog](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Postulate, "How Superhuman Used a Four-Question Survey"](https://postulate.us/@samsonzhang/p/2021-04-15-How-Superhuman-Used-a-Four-Question-oGqEg8zUriGRekTfnBUHwD).
- Starting score: 22% "very disappointed". Source: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Founder Labs](https://writings.founderlabs.io/p/how-superhuman-went-from-22-to-58).
- Segmentation method: group the responses by their answer to Q1, then assign a persona to each respondent. Use the "very disappointed" group as the lens for defining the target market, rather than narrowing by assumption, so "the data can speak for itself". Narrowing to the personas that were very disappointed raised the score from 22% to 33% in Vohra's telling. One secondary summary says 32%, a minor discrepancy. Sources: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Hustle Badger case study](https://www.hustlebadger.com/what-do-product-teams-do/superhuman-product-market-fit-case-study/) (secondary).
- The engine centers on the "high-expectation customer" (HXC): ask very-disappointed users what they love, ask the others what holds them back, then repeat the loop to sharpen focus on HXCs. Source: [David Cummings, "Running the Product/Market Fit Engine the Superhuman Way" (Oct 2020)](https://davidcummings.org/2020/10/10/running-the-product-market-fit-engine-the-superhuman-way/).
- Handling "somewhat disappointed" users: split them by whether the main benefit (for Superhuman, speed) resonates. Per the extract, Vohra wrote: "Somewhat disappointed users for whom speed was not the main benefit: we opted to politely disregard them, as our main benefit did not resonate. Even if we built everything they wanted, they would be unlikely to fall in love with the product." Source: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/).
- Outcome: the score reached 58% "very disappointed". The extract puts this at about a year after starting. A secondary headline says "in 9 months". The timing conflict is unresolved. Sources: [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); contrast [Founder Labs](https://writings.founderlabs.io/p/how-superhuman-went-from-22-to-58).
- Secondary advice: "Build only for them [very-disappointed segment] until you hit 40% 'very disappointed.' Then scale." Source: [IdeaPlan](https://www.ideaplan.io/case-studies/superhuman-product-market-fit) (secondary).

**Retention curve flattening as the core behavioral PMF signal**
- Secondary summaries attribute to Brian Balfour (Reforge) the definition that a retention curve is "flat" when a cohort stops losing people from one period to the next. The same summaries say a flat curve alone does not justify scaling: you also need cohort LTV to cover acquisition cost. They also warn that new channels bring users with different intent, so the plateau must be re-checked by channel. Sources: [Manuel Fusco, "Retention Curve Analysis: Find the Plateau Before You Scale"](https://manuelfusco.substack.com/p/retention-curve-plateau-before-scaling); [SuperMe.ai, "How do you know your retention curve has flattened?"](https://www.superme.ai/t/startup-growth/how-to-know-your-retention-curve-has-flattened). Both are secondary paraphrases, not Balfour's own text.

**Lenny Rachitsky and Casey Winters: "What is good retention" (Lenny's Newsletter Issue 29, a survey of practitioners)**
- User retention benchmarks, good / great: consumer social about 25% / 45%; consumer transactional about 30% / 50%; consumer SaaS about 40% / 70%; SMB/mid-market SaaS about 60% / 80%; enterprise SaaS about 70% / 90%. Sources: [Lenny's Newsletter, "What is good retention"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-good-retention-issue-29); [Casey Winters, "What Is Good Retention: An Exhaustive Benchmark Study with Lenny Rachitsky"](https://www.caseyaccidental.com/p/what-is-good-retention-an-exhaustive-benchmark-study-with-lenny-rachitsky).
- Time window: a secondary summary defines this user retention as "the percentage of users who signed up and are still active six months later". Source: [Founders Fuel summary](https://foundersfuel.beehiiv.com/p/good-great-retention) (secondary; the primary wording was not retrieved).
- Net revenue retention, consumer SaaS: about 55% good / 80% great. Source: [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-good-retention-issue-29).
- The post has a section titled "Disclaimer: why it may be ok for your retention to be low", so the benchmarks are meant to be read as context-dependent. Source: [Lenny's Newsletter section anchor](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/i/465580/disclaimer-why-it-may-be-ok-for-your-retention-to-be-low).

**D1/D7/D30 (Andrew Chen and Quettra, 2015; historical)**
- Data covered more than 125M Android phones (Quettra; CEO Ankit Jain). The average app loses 77% of its DAUs within the first 3 days after install and 90% within 30 days. Top apps have higher D1 retention and much stronger absolute D30, "but the falloff from D1 to D30 is about the same as other apps". Source: [Andrew Chen, "New data shows losing 80% of mobile users is normal, and why the best apps do better" (2015)](https://andrewchen.com/new-data-shows-why-losing-80-of-your-mobile-users-is-normal-and-that-the-best-apps-do-much-better/).
- The "80%" headline is loose. The post's own figure is 77% of DAUs by day 3. Secondary sources also disagree on whether the figures are DAU-based or install-based. Source: [Andrew Chen (2015)](https://andrewchen.com/new-data-shows-why-losing-80-of-your-mobile-users-is-normal-and-that-the-best-apps-do-much-better/); [Android Headlines coverage (Mar 2016)](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2016/03/losing-80-of-your-userbase-in-launch-week-is-normal.html).

**Smile curves**
- a16z on marketplaces: cohort curves "may 'smile' (curve back up) as churned users reactivate or retained users continue to expand". Source: [a16z, "GMV Retention: The Marketplace Metric Most Ignore"](https://a16z.com/gmv-retention-the-marketplace-metric-most-ignore/).
- a16z on consumer subscription: retaining "30–40% of users and revenue by the end of the first year is considered 'best practice'". The fastest-growing consumer AI companies now show revenue retention above 100%, meaning a cohort's revenue grows over time, driven by usage-based pricing and workplace adoption. Source: [a16z, "The Great Expansion: A New Era of Consumer Software"](https://a16z.com/the-great-expansion-a-new-era-of-consumer-software/).
- a16z splits cohort revenue retention into acquisition (M0–M3), retention (M3 to M6–9) and expansion (M9+) phases. It proposes an M12/M3 ratio that treats the month-3 base as the committed customer group, which keeps early "tourist" users from distorting the read. Source: [a16z, "Retention Is All You Need"](https://a16z.com/ai-retention-benchmarks/).
- A logo or user curve can only rise if churned users come back. Revenue curves can rise through expansion. So a "smile" on user cohorts means resurrection, and a smile on revenue cohorts can mean expansion. Source: [Churnkey, "Customer retention curve"](https://churnkey.co/blog/retention-curves/) (secondary vendor).

### Inferences
- **Encodable PMF rule (survey).** PMF_survey = share of "very disappointed" among eligible respondents, where eligible means at least 2 core-action uses in the last 14 days. Compute it overall and for the top persona segment. Show "PMF signal" when the segment score is at least 40%. Always show sample size and a 95% confidence interval. The interval half-width is about ±15pp at n=40 (1.96 × √(0.4·0.6/40)) and about ±10pp at n=100. A 34% score on 40 responses is statistically indistinguishable from 40%.
- **Encodable PMF rule (behavior).** Call a cohort curve "flat" when the period-over-period loss in active share is close to 0 (for example, under about 1–2pp per period, a tunable assumption) for at least 3 consecutive periods. Then compare the plateau level with Lenny's category "good" value at month 6. "Not flat" or "flat below good" should block scale-up recommendations (see §8).
- **Engine loop for Inroad.** Each survey cycle: (1) segment by persona and find the HXC; (2) mine Q3 (main benefit) among the very disappointed; (3) mine Q4 (improvements) only from "somewhat disappointed" users whose main benefit matches the HXC's; (4) ignore other somewhat and not-disappointed users; (5) re-survey and track the score as a time series.
- The Chen/Quettra finding that top and average apps decay at similar rates after D1 implies the biggest early lever is D0/D1 (first session and first-day value). This matches the RevenueCat finding that 55% of 3-day-trial cancellations happen on Day 0 (§5).
- For AI-era consumer products, Inroad should default to M3 as the denominator for retention reads (the a16z M12/M3 approach) and flag "tourist" inflation in M0–M2.

## 2. Engagement metrics: DAU/MAU, L7/L28 power user curves, session frequency by category

### Takeaway
The a16z/Andrew Chen Power User Curve is the preferred engagement diagnostic over DAU/MAU. It is a histogram of users by days active per week (L7) or month (L28/L30). A "smile" (a hump of hardcore users at the right end) and a rightward shift over time are the healthy signals. DAU/MAU heuristics are widely repeated (about 10–20% typical, 50%+ exceptional for social or messaging), but I could not trace them to a primary source. Treat them as folklore-grade.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: power user curves "are commonly called L30 charts for 30 days of use, or L7 charts for 7 days of use", a histogram of users' engagement by the total number of days they were active in a period. Source: [Andrew Chen, "The Power User Curve" (2018, with Li Jin; also published at a16z)](https://andrewchen.com/power-user-curve/); [Andrew Chen's tweet announcing the essay](https://x.com/andrewchen/status/1026523654425903107?lang=en).
- Shape: a healthy curve has a cluster at the low end plus a hardcore group at the top. Chen describes "a group of people who just use it 28 days out of 28 days". Jeff Jordan calls the shape a "smile". Source: [a16z Podcast transcript on andrewchen.com](https://andrewchen.com/a16z-podcast/).
- Several secondary sources say the Facebook growth team coined the "L30" label. Source: [Wudpecker guide](https://www.wudpecker.io/blog/measure-engagement-with-the-power-user-curve-comprehensive-guide) (secondary).
- Over time: "a more right leaning power user curve" across cohorts (users moving into higher-frequency buckets) signals a real network effect. Source: [a16z, "16 Ways to Measure Network Effects"](https://a16z.com/16-ways-to-measure-network-effects/).
- L7 vs L30: weekly curves suit products tied to the workweek, where the smile shifts toward days 1–5. Source: [Wudpecker guide](https://www.wudpecker.io/blog/measure-engagement-with-the-power-user-curve-comprehensive-guide) (secondary).
- DAU/MAU heuristics, all secondary with conflicting framings. The specific figure-to-page mapping across these aggregators was not verified:
  - 20% is the line where a product crosses into "habit" use and 50% is "utility", attributed to Andrew Chen and Facebook's early growth team. No primary found.
  - "The standard DAU/MAU ratio is 10–20% with only a handful of companies over 50%", attributed to Sequoia via Geckoboard.
  - Mixpanel's average across SaaS B2B and B2C apps is reportedly 13%, with B2C social at about 20–50%.
  - Consumer (e.g., Facebook-like) products: average about 30%, good 40%, great 50%.
  - Sources: [MetricHQ](https://www.metrichq.org/saas/dau-mau-ratio/); [June stickiness benchmark](https://help.june.so/en/articles/4752354-stickiness-benchmark); [Daymark](https://www.usedaymark.io/metrics/dau-mau-stickiness); [vmobify DAU/MAU benchmarks 2026](https://vmobify.com/blog/dau-mau-stickiness-benchmarks); [Wall Street Prep](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/product-stickiness/).

### Inferences
- **Encodable engagement rule.** Pick the window from the product's natural frequency. Use L28 for daily-intent products (social, messaging, games, habit apps) and L7 or weekly-active for weekly-intent products. For low-frequency transactional products (travel, e-commerce), skip DAU/MAU and track purchases per active customer per 30/90/365 days (see §5).
- Track the share of users in the top buckets each month (for example, the L28 ≥ 20-day share or the L7 ≥ 5-day share) as a "power user share" KPI. Alert when it trends down across successive monthly cohorts, even if MAU grows. Growth from low-engagement users can hide a deteriorating core.
- Show DAU/MAU only for daily-intent categories, and label any 20% / 50% guidance as "heuristic, unverified" in the product.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable, primary, category-level session-frequency benchmarks (sessions per user per day/week by category). Vendor reports from Mixpanel and Amplitude could not be retrieved.
- The original a16z Power User Curve essay was not fetched, so no exact numeric examples from it (e.g., the share of Facebook users at L30) are included.

## 3. Activation: defining the aha moment, verifying folklore numbers, time-to-value, onboarding, activation-rate benchmarks

### Takeaway
Lenny Rachitsky and Yuriy Timen's survey of 500+ products gives the main activation-rate benchmark: an average of 34% and a median of 25% overall (36% / 30% for SaaS only). Consumer transactional products (marketplaces, e-commerce) sit lower because their milestone is a first purchase. Two famous aha numbers trace back to their founders. Facebook's "7 friends in 10 days" is attributed to Chamath Palihapitiya (Growth Hackers Conference 2012, per secondary accounts). Slack's "2,000 messages" was stated by Stewart Butterfield to First Round. Both are simplified rules of thumb, not published analyses, and secondary retellings often distort them.

### Cited Findings
- Activation rate definition: activation rate = [users who hit the activation milestone] / [users who completed the signup flow]. The milestone is the "aha moment" that predicts long-term retention. Survey of 500+ products with Yuriy Timen: average 34%, median 25%. SaaS only (excluding marketplaces, e-commerce and DTC): average 36%, median 30%. Marketplaces and e-commerce are lowest because their milestone is typically "the first transaction (i.e. spending money)". Sources: [Lenny Rachitsky on LinkedIn, "What is a good activation rate" (post ID decodes to 2022-10-25)](https://rw.linkedin.com/posts/lennyrachitsky_what-is-a-good-activation-rate-activity-6990690292412665856-S_du?trk=public_profile_like_view); [Lenny thread via TweetHunter](https://tweethunter.io/thread-finder/lennysan/here-are-hot-off-the-press-benchmarks-for-good-and-great-activation-rates).
- Companion method post: [Lenny Rachitsky, "How to determine your activation metric" (post ID decodes to 2022-11-08)](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/lennyrachitsky_how-to-determine-your-activation-metric-activity-6995811135404855297-Gh86).
- Third-party restatements give other numbers (e.g., "bottom quartile <25%, top PLG 40–50%", "20–40% typical, 50–60% top quartile"). These mix sources and do not match the original. Sources: [Jimo](https://jimo.ai/blog/conducting-effective-benchmarking-analysis-a-step-by-step-guide); [ProductQuant](https://productquant.dev/blog/saas-activation-rate-benchmarks/) (treat with caution).
- **Facebook "7 friends in 10 days".** A Startup Archive excerpt quotes Chamath: "The single biggest thing we realized was to get any individual to 7 friends in 10 days" (no date or venue in the excerpt). Source: [Startup Archive](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/chamath-palihapitiya-on-the-growth-principles-that-got-facebook-to-billions-of-users). Secondary accounts place it at the Growth Hackers Conference in 2012, found by comparing cohorts that became engaged with cohorts that did not. Source: [Ryan Gum summary](https://ryangum.com/chamath-palihapitiya-how-we-put-facebook-on-the-path-to-1-billion-users/) (secondary). Chamath's general method: "start with a broad cross-section of engaged users" and work backward through the pathways they took. Source: [Startup Archive on X](https://x.com/StartupArchive_/status/1732372423603814704).
- Critique: aha moments "shouldn't be viewed as scientific tipping points; they're often round numbers picked in the middle of a range of possibilities". Source: [Mode, "Facebook's 'Aha' Moment Was Simpler Than You Think"](https://mode.com/blog/facebook-aha-moment-simpler-than-you-think/) (per extract).
- **Slack "2,000 messages".** Butterfield, in First Round Review: any team that has exchanged 2,000 messages in its history "has actually tried Slack", and "93% of those customers are still using Slack today". He called 2,000 messages the "magic number". Source: [First Round Review, "From 0 to $1B – Slack's Founder Shares Their Epic Launch Strategy"](https://review.firstround.com/from-0-to-1b-slacks-founder-shares-their-epic-launch-strategy/). The same quote appears in Andrew Chen's *The Cold Start Problem*: [Goodreads highlight](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/56962799-the-cold-start-problem/84411704-mikko-ikola/435bc888-c6a7-45f1-83c2-6c66617e1ab2).
- Distortion warning: some secondary sources restate the Slack figure as "93% conversion to long-term paid users". The primary is about continued use, not payment. Source: compare [IdeaPlan Slack case study](https://www.ideaplan.io/case-studies/slack-product-led-growth) against [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/from-0-to-1b-slacks-founder-shares-their-epic-launch-strategy/).
- Time-to-value signal from subscription data: 55% of all 3-day-trial cancellations happen on Day 0. Source: [RevenueCat, State of Subscription Apps 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps).

### Inferences
- **Encodable activation-event discovery (Chamath/Lenny method).** (1) Pick the retention outcome, e.g., active in week 4 or week 8, or a second purchase within 60 days for transactional products. (2) For each candidate early action (count × time window, e.g., "≥N core actions within D days"), compute the retention of users above versus below the threshold, plus coverage (the share of retained users who hit it). (3) Choose the threshold near the elbow where the retention lift saturates while coverage stays high. (4) Re-validate on a later cohort. Show famous numbers only as illustrations, labeled "simplified rule of thumb".
- Default activation-rate bands for Inroad (consumer), anchored to Lenny/Timen: under 25% means below median; 25–34% means median-to-average; above 34% means above average. For transactional or marketplace products, expect lower numbers and benchmark first-transaction rate separately.
- The Day-0 cancellation concentration means Inroad's weekly review should always include a first-session or first-day funnel (signup → first core action → aha), with time-to-aha as a median and p75.

### Gaps
- Not verified in this session (search budget exhausted): Twitter/X's "follow ~30 accounts" activation story (usually attributed to Josh Elman), Dropbox's "one file in one folder on one device", Zynga/D1 folklore, and other famous aha numbers. Treat them as unverified.
- No sourced consumer-specific time-to-value benchmarks or onboarding best-practice data (e.g., the effect of shortening onboarding steps) were found.
- No category-split consumer activation benchmarks (social vs subscription vs e-commerce) beyond the Lenny/Timen aggregate were found.

## 4. Growth accounting, the Quick Ratio, cohort basics, North Star metric selection

### Takeaway
Jonathan Hsu's (Social Capital) growth accounting splits each period's actives into new, retained, resurrected and churned users. The Quick Ratio, (new + resurrected) / churned, must exceed 1 to grow. Hsu calls 1–1.5 "so-so" for consumer, about 1.5 roughly average, and above 4 the preference for enterprise SaaS. Amplitude's North Star Playbook supplies the selection framework: a value-delivered metric, decomposed into breadth, depth, frequency and efficiency inputs, and chosen according to which "game" the product plays (attention, transaction or productivity).

### Cited Findings
- Growth accounting definitions (user version): new, retained, resurrected and churned users per period. Quick Ratio = (new + resurrected) / churned. It must be greater than 1 for the product to grow, "otherwise churn is overwhelming growth". Source: [Jonathan Hsu, "Diligence at Social Capital Part 1: Accounting for User Growth"](https://medium.com/swlh/diligence-at-social-capital-part-1-accounting-for-user-growth-4a8a449fddfc).
- Consumer thresholds: Hsu's example app ran between 1 and 1.5, which he called "a so-so situation for a consumer app". He notes most consumer apps lack a strong mechanism to bring users back month after month, so their ratios usually sit just above 1. He also says 1.5 is about average for a consumer business but weak for subscription revenue: subscription businesses such as Spotify or Netflix should show higher ratios because they churn less. Sources: [Hsu Part 1](https://medium.com/swlh/diligence-at-social-capital-part-1-accounting-for-user-growth-4a8a449fddfc); [Hsu, "The quick ratio is a good standard way of measuring…"](https://medium.com/@jonathanhsu/the-quick-ratio-is-a-good-standard-way-of-measuring-the-relative-growth-vs-99eed71c2918).
- SaaS thresholds: "for enterprise SaaS companies, we prefer to invest in companies with a quick ratio greater than 4". From a 2015 deck: if the ratio is below 2, "your churn is probably too high". Sources: [Hsu Part 2: Accounting for Revenue Growth](https://medium.com/swlh/diligence-at-social-capital-part-2-accounting-for-revenue-growth-551fa07dd972); [Jonathan Hsu, WMD 2015 deck, "Measuring For Growth"](https://www.slideshare.net/500startups/wmd-2015-jonathan-hsu) (2015).
- Caveat: this metric is not the finance quick ratio, which measures liquidity. Source: [Hsu Part 1](https://medium.com/swlh/diligence-at-social-capital-part-1-accounting-for-user-growth-4a8a449fddfc). Hsu later extended the framework into the "8-ball" and "GAAP for Startups": [Hsu, Epilogue](https://medium.com/swlh/diligence-at-social-capital-epilogue-introducing-the-8-ball-and-gaap-for-startups-7ab215c378bc).
- Amplitude North Star input heuristic: breadth, depth, frequency and efficiency. Instacart example: North Star = total monthly items received on time. Breadth = customers placing orders each month; depth = items per order; frequency = orders per customer per month; efficiency = % of orders delivered on time. Source: [Amplitude, "The North Star Playbook" (PDF)](https://info.amplitude.com/rs/138-CDN-550/images/Amplitude-The-North-Star-Playbook.pdf).
- The three "games": attention, transaction and productivity. Transaction-game examples include total purchases completed and average purchases per Prime subscriber. Attention example: a newspaper's North Star of "regular readers", meaning subscribers consuming more than 7 articles per month. Amplitude's own North Star is Weekly Learning Users, defined as users who experience at least one "learning moment" per week. A Burger King squad prioritized "mobile order coupons" to drive the frequency input. Sources: [Amplitude North Star Playbook](https://info.amplitude.com/rs/138-CDN-550/images/Amplitude-The-North-Star-Playbook.pdf); [Amplitude North Star how-to guide 2024](https://info.amplitude.com/rs/138-CDN-550/images/North-Star_how-to-Guide_2024.pdf). The playbook is about 5 years old, and some extract text was garbled.
- Third-party glossaries add streaming and marketplace examples that are not from Amplitude; do not attribute them to the Playbook. Source: [BoardSnap glossary](https://boardsnap.ai/glossary/amplitude-north-star/).

### Inferences
- **Encodable growth-accounting identity.** Actives_t = Actives_{t−1} − churned_t + new_t + resurrected_t, where retained_t = Actives_{t−1} − churned_t. Inroad can render this as a weekly or monthly waterfall in place of the B2B deal-stage pipeline. Use weekly granularity for daily or weekly-intent products and monthly for lower-frequency ones (Hsu's examples are monthly).
- **Quick Ratio bands for consumer (inference built on Hsu).** Under 1 = shrinking (red). 1–1.5 = typical or "so-so" consumer (amber). Above 1.5 = above average for consumer (green-ish). For consumer subscription, expect higher and treat ≥2 as the healthier bar, since Hsu says below 2 signals too much churn in recurring-revenue contexts. Show the QR next to the gross churn rate, because a high QR from heavy paid acquisition can mask churn.
- **North Star selection rule for Inroad.** Choose the game. Attention: e.g., weekly users completing at least X core sessions. Transaction: e.g., weekly or monthly completed purchases or orders by repeat customers. Productivity: e.g., weekly users completing at least one meaningful task. Then decompose the North Star into breadth × depth × frequency × efficiency and attach each experiment to one input. Inroad's "one headline number" should be the North Star, with the Quick Ratio and cohort plateau as guardrails.
- **Cohort basics to encode.** Cohort by signup or first-value week. Measure the share active in each later period (unbounded or bracketed). Keep the cohort definition and activity definition fixed across weeks so trends are comparable.

## 6. Experimentation and cadence: weekly growth meeting, experiment velocity, ICE/RICE, minimum sample sizes and when not to A/B test

### Takeaway
Sean Ellis's weekly growth meeting (from *Hacking Growth*, 2017, and his SaaStr 2016 talk) is a 60-minute agenda in five blocks: metrics, last week's tests, learnings, next tests and the idea pipeline. Ideas are scored with ICE. RICE (Intercom) is the more quantitative alternative. Ronny Kohavi's rule of thumb is that A/B testing "doesn't work out" statistically below tens of thousands of users, and that about 200,000 users is where detecting roughly 5% changes becomes practical. Most pre-seed and seed consumer startups should not run classic A/B tests on retention metrics.

### Cited Findings
- **Weekly growth meeting agenda** (Ellis): held on Tuesdays and run by the growth lead. 15 min: metrics review and update the focus area ("growth metrics, issues and opportunities"). 10 min: review last week's testing activity or sprint. 15 min: key lessons from analyzed experiments. 15 min: select growth tests for the current cycle. 5 min: check the growth of the idea pipeline. Sources: [Graham Mann, Hacking Growth notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/hacking-growth-sean-ellis); [Sean Ellis, "Building a Company-Wide Growth Culture", SaaStr Annual 2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/seanellis/building-a-companywide-growth-culture-saastr-annual-2016/14-Weekly_Growth_Meeting_Agenda_15); [SaaStr video and transcript](https://www.saastr.com/sean-ellis-founder-ceo-of-growthhackers-building-a-company-wide-growth-culture-video-transcript/).
- Pre-meeting prep: team members check finished tests from the previous cycle, gather takeaways and nominate ideas. Ideas are ranked with the ICE score (Impact, Confidence, Ease). Source: [Shortform, Hacking Growth summary](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/hacking-growth-pdf-sean-ellis-and-morgan-brown).
- **RICE** (Sean McBride, Intercom blog; sources disagree on the year, 2017 or 2018). Score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort. Impact scale: 3 massive, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low, 0.25 minimal. Confidence: 100% high, 80% medium, 50% low; below 50% is a "total moonshot". Effort is in person-months. Sources: [Tempo guide](https://www.tempo.io/guides/rice-score-prioritization-framework-product-management); [ProdCamp](https://www.prodcamp.com/blog/the-rice-prioritization-framework-deploy-it-master-it) (secondary; the original Intercom post was not retrieved).
- **Kohavi on minimum scale** (Lenny's Podcast, "The ultimate guide to A/B testing", July 2023): "Unless you have at least tens of thousands of users, the math, the statistics just don't work out for most of the metrics that you're interested in." Source: [Lenny's Podcast Wiki, A/B Testing](https://lennyrachitsky.wiki/articles/ab-testing). Detecting changes of about 5% generally needs around 200,000 users, "a magical place". Below that, focus on culture, platform and integration. Sources: [Medium, "Lessons from Ronny Kohavi"](https://medium.com/@shenjiejie2017/lessons-from-ronny-kohavi-on-a-b-testing-039aabed6543); [podcast summary (PodPulse)](https://podpulse.ai/podcast/lennys-podcast-product-growth-career/lennys-podcast-product-growth-career-the-ultimate-guide-to-ab-testing-ronny-kohavi-airbnb-microsoft-amazon). One LinkedIn recap instead pairs a 5% effect with 10k users and a 1% effect with 200k, so the secondary sources conflict: [LinkedIn listening notes (post ID decodes to 2023-10-10)](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/alex-friedman-588680139_friedswritings-activity-7117504644506865665-SC1m).
- GrowthBook's notes from a Kohavi webinar: his rule of thumb is "never set an MDE above 5%" (realistic effects are small). Their sample-size table lists about 100,000 users for a 10% baseline conversion. With about 10,000 users "you are nowhere unless your metric converts at around 50%". Source: [GrowthBook, "Making A/B Tests You Can Actually Trust"](https://www.growthbook.io/blog/lessons-learned-from-ronny-kohavi-and-luke-sonnet-running-trustworthy-experiments).
- Lenny published "An easy rule of thumb for when your startup should start running A/B tests…" (post ID decodes to 2023-07-28). The content was not extracted. Source: [Lenny Rachitsky on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/lennyrachitsky_an-easy-rule-of-thumb-for-when-your-startup-activity-7090729542834679808-6_eU).

### Inferences
- **Sample-size rule of thumb to encode (standard statistics, Lehr's approximation).** Users needed per variant ≈ 16 · p(1−p) / δ², where p = baseline rate and δ = absolute lift to detect, at α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power. Worked values computed for this note:
  - p = 10%, 5% relative lift (0.5pp): about 57,600 per arm, about 115k total. This matches GrowthBook's "about 100k".
  - p = 10%, 10% relative (1pp): about 14,400 per arm.
  - p = 10%, 20% relative (2pp): about 3,600 per arm.
  - p = 30%, 10% relative (3pp): about 3,700 per arm.
  - p = 30%, 20% relative (6pp): about 930 per arm.
  - p = 25%, 30% relative (7.5pp): about 530 per arm.
- **"Should you A/B test?" gate for Inroad.** Compute n per arm for the target metric and a realistic MDE (default 10% relative, never assume more than 20–30% unless it is a big redesign). If (eligible weekly users ÷ number of arms) × 4 weeks < n, say "don't A/B test". Instead: ship big swings, compare before/after cohorts, use holdouts only for high-stakes changes, and lean on qualitative research. Kohavi's tens-of-thousands floor and roughly 200k "magical place" are the narrative anchors.
- Never A/B test on lagging retention metrics (e.g., M3) at seed scale. Test on leading indicators (activation rate, D1/D7) with large expected effects.
- **Weekly cadence to encode** (adapted from Ellis): metrics and focus area (North Star, input metrics, Quick Ratio, newest cohort plateau) → last week's experiments (shipped / not shipped) → learnings (including user-conversation insights) → pick next experiments by ICE (simple) or RICE (when reach varies a lot) → idea backlog health. The meeting length is Ellis's 60 minutes; the ordering is his.

## 7. User research for consumer founders: conversation cadence, recruiting, incentives, Mom Test rules, churned-user and JTBD switch interviews

### Takeaway
The best-sourced cadence floor is Teresa Torres's definition of continuous discovery: "at a minimum, weekly touchpoints with customers by the team building the product". Interviews should collect specific past stories, not hypotheticals. The Bob Moesta/Chris Spiek JTBD switch interview models switching as Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit and interviews recent switchers along a timeline from first thought to purchase. Incentive benchmarks are vendor-sourced: roughly $25–$75 for a 30-minute consumer interview.

### Cited Findings
- Torres's definition of continuous discovery: "At a minimum, weekly touchpoints with customers by the team building the product, where they're conducting small research activities in pursuit of a desired product outcome." Interview someone who actually has the need, and ground the interview in specific past stories, because hypothetical questions ("what do you like to watch?") often don't reflect what really happens. Source: [Maze interview with Teresa Torres](https://maze.co/blog/making-better-product-decisions-with-teresa-torres/).
- A product trio might schedule one or two customer conversations every week. Source: [Simpliaxis on continuous discovery](https://www.simpliaxis.com/resources/continuous-discovery) (secondary).
- **JTBD four forces** (Moesta and Spiek): a customer switches when Push (problem with the current situation) + Pull (appeal of the new solution) > Anxiety (about the new solution) + Habit (of the present). Teams over-focus on pull while anxiety and habit kill many switches. Source: [jobstobedone.org, "The Four Forces"](https://jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces/); [jobstobedone.org, "Habit of the Present"](https://jobstobedone.org/radio/forces-friday-habit-of-the-present/).
- Switch-interview technique: interview people who recently switched (not prospects), because recency matters and memory degrades. Reconstruct the timeline from first thought to purchase, not just the final decision. Sources: [Business of Software, Bob Moesta, "Live Jobs to be Done Case Studies"](https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/live-jobs-to-be-done-case-studies/); [Koji, "Switch Interviews: The JTBD Method"](https://www.koji.so/docs/switch-interviews-jtbd-method) (secondary vendor).
- A five-stage timeline (first thought → passive looking → active looking → deciding → consuming) appears in one practitioner guide, attributed to a Perspective AI JTBD guide rather than to Moesta directly. Source: [Young Urban Project](https://www.youngurbanproject.com/customer-interviews-for-positioning/) (secondary; not confirmed as Moesta's exact breakdown).
- **Incentives** (vendor guides; ranges conflict): $15–$25 or $25–$50 for a 30-minute general-consumer interview in some guides, $50–$75 in others, and $50–$150 for moderated 30–60-minute interviews. One guide says incentive effectiveness for consumer audiences generally plateaus around $50. Gift cards are taxable income in the US. One guide says the 1099-MISC reporting threshold rises to $2,000 for payments made from 2026 (verify with a tax source). Sources: [Lyssna](https://www.lyssna.com/blog/incentives-for-research-participants/); [Tremendous](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/always-send-research-incentives-a-guide/); [Koji incentives benchmarks 2026](https://www.koji.so/docs/research-participant-incentives); [BuildBetter](https://blog.buildbetter.ai/how-to-recruit-and-pay-research-participants/); [Hoppier](https://www.hoppier.com/blog/survey-incentives). User Interviews' own incentives page covers payment methods but gave no benchmark figure in results: [User Interviews](https://www.userinterviews.com/incentives).

### Inferences
- **Encodable cadence.** A floor of at least 1 user conversation per week (Torres minimum). For pre-PMF consumer founders, Inroad could default to a higher target, for example 3–5 per week split across new activated users, power users, and churned or non-activated users. This target is an inference, not a sourced benchmark; make it configurable.
- **Recruiting defaults.** Use in-app or email prompts triggered by behavior: just activated, just hit the power-user threshold, just churned or cancelled, just switched from a competitor. Offer $25–$50 gift cards for 30 minutes with consumers (the midpoint of the vendor ranges, near the reported $50 plateau).
- **Interview scripts Inroad can generate.** (1) Switch interview for new and recently converted users: timeline plus push, pull, anxiety and habit. (2) Churn interview: the same timeline run in reverse, i.e. what triggered leaving and what they use now. (3) PMF follow-ups to Superhuman Q2–Q4 responses. All of these should ask about specific past behavior, never "would you use…".

## 8. Sequencing rules: retention before acquisition, when to start paid acquisition, when to hire growth

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree on the order: find PMF (survey plus a flattening retention curve) before investing in growth tactics, and do not scale acquisition, especially paid, until retention plateaus and unit economics work. Sean Ellis's startup pyramid (PMF → Transition to Growth → Growth) and Brian Balfour's "retention beats acquisition" are the clearest statements. I found no sourced numeric threshold for "start paid acquisition" or "hire your first growth person" for consumer.

### Cited Findings
- Ellis's startup pyramid has three layered stages: Product/Market Fit (the base), Transition to Growth, then Growth. Pursuing growth before PMF is working on an unsolvable problem, and a product without PMF can't be grown sustainably. Sources: [SaaSquatch recap of Sean Ellis](https://www.saasquatch.com/blog/sean-ellis-recap-on-the-stages-of-growth/); [Alchemist Accelerator, Sean Ellis](https://blog.alchemistaccelerator.com/learn-how-to-grow-your-startup-with-sean-ellis-the-father-of-growth-hacking).
- Ellis's diagnosis: "If you're not growing, is it an acquisition problem, is it a conversion problem, or is it a core product problem? Two of those three can be addressed by growth hacking. One cannot." He has also warned against leaning on paid acquisition to force growth because it is too short-term. Sources: [Mixpanel, "Sean Ellis on growth hacking, company-wide"](https://mixpanel.com/blog/sean-ellis-growth-hacking-2); [SaaSquatch](https://www.saasquatch.com/blog/sean-ellis-recap-on-the-stages-of-growth/).
- Balfour (Intercom podcast, about 6 years old): teams feel pressure to scale channels even though they "might have a bunch of retention problems". You "probably only have enough resources to work on retention or scaling up acquisition". "The more customers you retain ends up driving your acquisition loops, viral-driven loop, or even a paid loop." Source: [Intercom, "Brian Balfour on creating meaningful growth"](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/podcast-brian-balfour-on-creating-meaningful-growth). Balfour and Andrew Chen's Reforge research reportedly found the top company in a category "always had the best retention by far". Source: [GrowthTalent, "Brian Balfour on Why Retention Beats Acquisition"](https://www.growthtalent.org/insights/brian-balfour-on-why-retention-beats-acquisition-every-time) (secondary).
- "Leaky bucket": the metaphor is usually credited to Andrew Chen, not Balfour. When acquisition costs are high (e.g., mobile), starting with retention avoids wasting budget on a leaky bucket. Source: [Koji, AARRR guide](https://www.koji.so/docs/aarrr-pirate-metrics-framework) (secondary).
- Before scaling, check that the retention plateau holds and that cohort LTV covers acquisition cost. Re-check the plateau by channel, because paid users differ in intent from early adopters. Source: [Manuel Fusco](https://manuelfusco.substack.com/p/retention-curve-plateau-before-scaling) (secondary).

### Inferences (encodable pushback rules for Inroad; thresholds are inferences built on the sources above)
- **Block or warn on "scale paid spend"** unless all of these hold: (1) the latest 3+ cohorts show a flattening curve (§1 definition); (2) the plateau is at or above Lenny's category "good" at month 6, or for subscription, annual-plan year-one retention ≥ about 27% (RevenueCat median), ideally 30–40% (a16z best practice); (3) the Sean Ellis score is ≥40% on n ≥ 40 in the target segment; (4) the Quick Ratio is above 1 without paid users, so organic growth is not being masked; (5) a paid test cohort is tracked separately and its own curve flattens; (6) LTV on the paid cohort covers CAC within the startup's payback tolerance (the tolerance is user-set; no sourced consumer number).
- **Diagnose before tactics** (Ellis's three-problem test). If activation and retention are below benchmark, label it a core product problem and route effort to product, user conversations and the activation event, not channels.
- **Growth hire.** Recommend a dedicated growth hire only after PMF signals (survey ≥40% and a flattened curve) plus at least one repeatable acquisition channel. This is an inference; no sourced threshold was found.

### Gaps
- Casey Winters's, Andrew Chen's and Reforge's specific guidance on when to hire a first growth person or build a growth team could not be retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- No primary source was found for numeric "start paid acquisition" thresholds for consumer (e.g., CAC payback months, a minimum organic share).
- The B2B-blog heuristic "below 10% [retention] means acquisition is pouring water into a leaky bucket" ([GrowthMethod](https://growthmethod.com/retention-beats-acquisition/) / [Manuel Fusco](https://manuelfusco.substack.com/p/retention-curve-plateau-before-scaling)) is not a consumer standard. It is excluded from the rules.

## 9. Seed/Series A investor expectations for consumer metrics (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
