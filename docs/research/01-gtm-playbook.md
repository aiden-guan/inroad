# Early-stage B2B GTM methodology for technical founders, from 0 to about 20 customers

**Research note:** the web-search budget ran out partway through. Most claims below come from primary sources: Google, Yahoo, Gong, Instantly, Belkins, Hunter, Lavender, First Round, Bessemer, Pillar, SaaStr and Growth Unhinged. Some details of YC talks (Koomen, Blomfield, Epstein) come from secondary summaries, and these are marked **[secondary]**. Numbers from vendors selling signal or sequencing tools are marked **[vendor]**. Treat both as directional.

---

## 0. The canonical playbook in 12 lines

1. **The founder sells the first 10–20 deals.** Every source agrees. Hiring a "head of GTM" before there is a repeatable playbook is the most expensive common mistake.
2. **Start from a falsifiable ICP hypothesis.** Define a group of companies, a named role, a painful obstacle and a promised improvement. Narrow it until it hurts.
3. **Source pipeline outward by trust.** Order: personal network → strategic cold outbound → investor networks → communities → content → press → launches.
4. **Give every outreach a real reason.** That means a public signal, interpreted into a problem. No signal, no send.
5. **Keep cold emails 40–100 words.** One problem, one interest-based question, a 1–4 word subject, 3–5 touches, then switch channel.
6. **Every call is discovery first.** Use SPICED or MEDDICC-lite. Quantify impact and find the critical event. A meeting without a dated next step is a failed meeting.
7. **Charge from the start.** Use paid, time-boxed pilots with written success criteria, or design partners with a written conversion date and price. Long unpaid "design partnerships" are where startups stall.
8. **Price on the customer's value, not your cost.** Discount only in exchange for something: a case study, a multi-year term, a fast signature.
9. **Map the buying committee early.** Start the security and legal paperwork early, and run a mutual action plan (MAP).
10. **Every week, review inputs, conversion rates, stuck deals and what you learned.** Disqualify fast. Only count qualified deals as pipeline.
11. **Protect deliverability.** Use authenticated domains, low volume, no bounces, complaints under 0.1%, and an easy opt-out.
12. **The first hire is a "pathfinder" founding AE (account executive), not a VP or head of GTM.** Hire after about 10 customers and once more than 20% of founder time goes to selling. Ideally hire two so you can compare.

### 1.2 Synthesis on the "business-grad head of GTM" anti-pattern
- **Consensus:** do not hire a VP or head of sales at seed. Sources: Jen Abel; Pillar; Lemkin (VP only after reps hit quota); Kazanjy (package the process first). One outlier: an a16z crypto playbook (2026, secondary) recommends a sales leader first. Treat it as the minority view.
- **Sequence:**
  1. The founder closes about 10–20 customers and documents ICP, pitch, objections and stage exit criteria.
  2. Hire 1–2 founding AEs ("pathfinders": figure-it-out factor, your persona, 18+ months of selling at a similar price point).
  3. Hire a VP when the AEs hit quota.
  4. Optionally bring in a GTM engineer or agency for signal and automation work in lower-ACV motions.
- **Product thesis:** the AI Head of GTM should not replace the founder in the room. It should do everything around the room: ICP and research, signal mining, drafting, call prep, debriefs, MAPs, proposals, pipeline hygiene, weekly reviews. It should also produce the documented playbook that makes the eventual first hire succeed.

## 2. ICP, buying committee, positioning and messaging

### 2.1 ICP template (encode as a structured object)
- **Hypothesis sentence** (Koomen): "[Company group] where [role] struggles with [obstacle] because [cause]; we deliver [measurable improvement]."
- **Firmographics:** industry or vertical, business model (B2B SaaS, marketplace, services…), employee band, funding stage and recency, geography, growth signals.
- **Technographics:** tools that must exist (integration points), tools that indicate the pain, competitor or complementary tools.
- **Situational triggers:** events that create urgency (section 4), each with a window in days.
- **Pains:** in the customer's own words, ranked by frequency and severity, each with an impact metric.
- **Buying committee and personas:** see 2.2.
- **Anti-ICP and disqualifiers:**
  - No owner for the problem.
  - Not spending money or time on it today (Jen Abel test).
  - Compliance gates you can't pass yet.
  - ACV below what the motion supports. Hale [secondary]: products under about $2K need to be self-serve.
- **Evidence ledger:** number of conversations confirming each pain or trigger, number who paid. Best-fit customers (Dunford) are the ones who understood the product fastest, bought, and got outsized value. Rebuild the ICP from them as soon as they exist, rather than averaging across all customers.
- **Segment lock:** one segment at a time (Jen Abel). If testing, run at most 2–3 ICP slices in parallel and compare reply and qualified rates. The slice counts are a heuristic.
- **Worked example (Retool-style):** Series A–C, raised in the last 6 months, operations-heavy (logistics, fintech, marketplaces), not SaaS. Personas: CTO and VP Operations. Trigger: hiring ops or internal-tools engineers. Pain: engineers pulled into building admin panels.

### 2.2 Buying-committee map
Template fields per deal (roles from Blomfield, MEDDICC and speedrun):

| Role | Who it usually is (seed-to-enterprise) | What they need | Questions to identify them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Champion | The person who feels the pain and has influence | A personal win plus ammunition to sell internally | "Who else cares about this being fixed?" "Would you walk your boss through this?" (test with a small ask: an intro, or an internal document) |
| Coach | Friendly, no power | Do not confuse with the champion | "If you wanted to buy this, what would you need to do?" (a vague answer suggests a coach) |
| Economic buyer | Owner of the budget (VP, CFO, CEO at small companies) | ROI and risk | "Whose budget does this come from?" "Who signed the last tool like this?" |
| Technical approver | CTO, eng lead, IT | Architecture, integration effort | "Who has to sign off technically?" |
| Security / IT / compliance | Security, GRC | SOC 2, data flow, DPA | "What does security review look like? What documents do you need?" |
| Legal / procurement | Legal, finance ops | Paper, terms, vendor onboarding | "What's the paper process after a yes? How long did the last one take?" |
| Users | The team doing the work daily | Workflow fit | "Who would use this every day?" |

- At under 100 employees there are often 1–3 people. Enterprise committees are larger: a commonly cited Forrester figure is 13 internal plus 9 external stakeholders [secondary].
- C-level executives are 30.2% less likely to reply to cold email (Gong: https://www.gong.io/blog/do-execs-really-reply-to-cold-email-here-s-what-the-data-says). Start with the champion persona and multi-thread up to the economic buyer.

### 2.3 Positioning: April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome*
Five components, decided in this order (https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-april-dunford.md):
1. **Competitive alternatives:** what the customer would do without you. Include the status quo, spreadsheets, hiring someone, building in-house and direct competitors.
2. **Unique attributes:** capabilities you have that the alternatives lack.
3. **Value and proof:** the outcome each attribute enables, clustered into 2–3 value themes, each with evidence.
4. **Best-fit customers:** the characteristics that make someone care a lot about that value.
5. **Market category:** the frame that makes the value obvious. Three styles:
   - **Head-to-head:** an existing category you can win.
   - **Big fish, small pond:** a subsegment of an existing category.
   - **New game:** create a category only if existing ones can't explain you and you can afford to educate the market.
- Optional: a relevant trend layered on top.
- Rules:
  - Prefer a familiar category to a novel label.
  - Positioning can't rescue an undifferentiated product.
  - Don't reuse investor language as the sales story.
  - Study wins and losses.
- Full process from the book, from my knowledge of it:
  1. Find the customers who love you.
  2. Form a team.
  3. Drop baggage.
  4. List the alternatives.
  5. Isolate unique attributes.
  6. Map attributes to value themes.
  7. Find who cares a lot.
  8. Pick the market frame.
  9. Layer a trend (optional).
  10. Capture and share.

### 2.4 Sales narrative: Dunford's *Sales Pitch*
(https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/a-step-by-step-guide-to-crafting, plus summaries)

**Setup phase**
1. **Insight:** a change in the world that reframes the buyer's problem.
2. **Alternatives:** honest pros and cons of each approach, including the status quo.
3. **Perfect world:** the purchase criteria an ideal solution would meet. These should line up with your differentiators.

**Follow-through phase**
4. **Introduce the product** in its category.
5. **Differentiated value:** 2–3 themes, each demonstrated through outcomes, not a feature tour.
6. **Proof:** customers, metrics, third parties, live demo on their data.
7. **Objections:** pre-empt the known ones.
8. **The ask:** a specific next step.

Gong data: keep the "about us" portion to about 2 minutes, because win rates drop sharply after that (https://www.gong.io/blog/winning-sales-conversations). Don't use FOMO tactics (Dunford).

### 2.5 Messaging hierarchy (encode as a tree)
- **L0, one-liner:** "[Product] is a [category] that helps [best-fit customer] [achieve outcome] without [main pain of alternatives]."
- **L1, value themes (2–3):** each tied to a differentiator vs. a named alternative.
- **L2, proof per theme:** a metric, customer story, demo moment or founder credential. No proof means don't claim it.
- **L3, persona translations:** champion (daily pain, time), economic buyer (dollars, risk, strategic goal), technical approver (effort, security), users (workflow).
- **L4, signal angles:** per trigger type, the implication sentence (section 4).
- **Language rules:**
  - Use customer words collected from calls. Josh Braun's example: Basecamp customers said "buried under email."
  - Ban buzzwords and vague ROI claims. Gong found these significantly reduce replies; the author specifically avoids "AI" and "platform."

**Targets for a founder doing hand-researched outbound (heuristic):**
- Reply rate of 10% or more. Meetings booked at 3–5% or more of people contacted.

- Measure replies, not opens. Apple Mail Privacy Protection makes open rates unreliable.

### 3.2 Cold-email rules the AI should enforce
1. **Length:** target 40–80 words, hard cap 100. Readable on a phone with no scrolling.
2. **Structure:**
   - Why you, why now: a signal or observation.
   - The problem it likely creates, in their words.
   - Credibility: a similar company's result, or founder expertise.
   - One interest-based question.
   - No product feature list.
3. **Subject:** 1–4 words, specific, referencing the trigger or problem. Should read like an internal email ("usage billing close", "EMEA forecasting"). No clickbait, no "Quick question".
4. **Real vs. fake personalization:**
   - **Fake:** a compliment or trivia unrelated to the problem ("Loved your post!", "Go Bears!").
   - **Real:** a premise that logically leads to the problem you solve.
   - **Test:** delete the first line. If the rest could be sent to anyone, the personalization was decorative.
   - Becc Holland's premise buckets, in rough order of response: (1) content the person wrote, (2) content they engaged with, (3) traits they list about themselves, (4) company news (M&A, hiring, website language), (5) everything else. Some premises signal timing and should not be mentioned in the message. ([secondary] https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-becc-holland/)
   - Caliber's distinction: personalization proves you did research; a point of view proves you understand the problem.
5. **CTA ladder:**
   - First touch: interest ("worth a look?", "is this on your radar?"), resource ("want the benchmark?"), or referral ("are you the right person?").
   - Offer specific times only after engagement.
   - No calendar link in the first email.
   - One CTA per email.
6. **Tone:**
   - "You" should outnumber "I/we".
   - Tentative and unsure ("might be off base"). Detached from the outcome (Braun).
   - Allow an easy "no".
7. **Founder advantage:**
   - Send from the founder's own name.
   - Ask for advice or feedback rather than a meeting (Zip's approach; Lavender's "Founder Card"; First Round's expert-interview framing).
   - Use the technical credential ("I built X at Y").
8. **Proof honesty:** never invent customers, metrics or "teams like yours" claims. If there is no proof yet, use founder expertise, a specific insight or data, or a design-partner invitation.
9. **Follow-ups:**
   - 3–5 total touches over about 14–21 days. Intervals: +2–3 days, then +4–7 days.
   - Each touch adds a new angle: second implication → social proof or story → neutral third-party insight → referral ask → breakup.
   - Never "just bumping this."
   - Then switch to LinkedIn or phone, and recycle after 60–90 days with a new signal.
   - Fewer touches for enterprise and executives.
10. **Never send identical emails** to several people at one company at the same time. It triggers backchannel comparison and deliverability problems (Lavender).

### 3.3 Five practitioner structures, paraphrased with original example copy
The examples are illustrative, for a hypothetical "Ledgerline" that reconciles usage-billing data for finance teams. Practitioner framework sources: https://lavender.ai/blog/sales-email-frameworks; https://www.11x.ai/guides/josh-braun-cold-outreach-method; Gong exec article above.

**A. Josh Braun "4T": Truth → Think → Third-party → Talk** (~80 words max; subject references the Truth)
- Truth: something observable and true about them.
- Think: a problem they may not have considered, stated neutrally.
- Third-party: what similar companies saw. Avoid "we're great".
- Talk: a low-friction ask that's easy to say no to.
- Example:
  - Subject: *usage pricing close*
  - Body: "Your pricing page switched to usage-based tiers in June. When usage events and invoices live in different systems, month-end close often becomes a spreadsheet reconciliation nobody owns. Two finance teams at similar-stage companies told us that's where most of their close time went. Worth comparing notes, or is this already handled?"

**B. Lavender "Mouse Trap": Observation → yes/no question** (1–2 sentences; first touch, DM or follow-up)
- The question implies your solution without naming it.
- Example: "Saw the revenue accountant role mentions 'owning usage reconciliation.' Is that still done by hand today?"

**C. Gong / Jen Allen-Knuth "Problem Prompter" for executives** (under 100 words)
- Strategic objective → problem blocking it → acknowledge the status quo → offer a new perspective instead of a meeting → low-pressure P.S.
- Example: "Your Q2 letter prioritized moving enterprise customers to consumption pricing. The usual blocker isn't pricing strategy, it's finance trusting the usage numbers enough to invoice from them. Most teams patch this with a monthly export plus an analyst. I put together a one-page breakdown of how three companies made usage data invoice-grade. Want it? P.S. If this isn't on your plate, who owns it?"

**D. "Vanilla Ice Cream" (Lavender) ≈ Observation → Problem → Proof → Interest** (the shape commonly attributed to 30MPC [secondary])
- One sentence each: observation; problem or insight; credibility (who you helped, result); solution; call to conversation.
- Example: "Noticed you're hiring two data engineers to 'own billing pipelines.' Teams usually start that hire after invoices and usage diverge for the first time. [Peer] closed that gap in 3 weeks without a new hire. Ledgerline matches usage events to invoices automatically. Open to seeing how?"

**E. "Founder Card" (Lavender) for zero logos and zero proof**
- Problem plus a low-pressure goal → explicitly asking for feedback → who I am and my credibility → the specific feedback wanted, and whether they're open to it.
- Example: "I'm building a tool for finance teams whose usage data and invoices don't match. Not selling anything yet; I'm trying to learn whether that pain is real at companies like yours. Before this I built Stripe billing infrastructure at [Co]. Would you be open to 20 minutes on how month-end close works for you? Happy to share what I've learned from 30 other finance leads."

**Follow-up library (Lavender)**
- **Thoughtful bump:** context plus "any thoughts?" in 1–3 sentences.
- **Neutral insight:** share a third-party resource, not your own blog, explain why it's relevant, optional soft ask.
- **Well-researched referral:** name the likely better contact and why. A personalized P.S. reportedly lifts replies 35%.
- **Breakup:** state why you reached out, assume the timing is off, ask whether it'll be a priority in about two quarters, say this is the last note.
- **Re-engage 3 ways:** a one-line question on whether the priority still matters; "something new on our side"; "something changed on your side".

**Five-touch sequence skeleton (Caliber POV-led):** pressure goes down, not up.
1. Strongest insight.
2. A second painful implication.
3. Social proof as a short story.
4. Low-pressure invite.
5. Permission to exit.

### 3.4 Deliverability rules (2025–2026)
- **Gmail, all senders:**
  - SPF or DKIM (DKIM ≥1024-bit, 2048 recommended), valid forward and reverse DNS, TLS, RFC 5322 formatting.
  - Spam rate below **0.3%**, with a target below **0.1%**. Above 0.1% already hurts inboxing.
  - Source: https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126
- **Gmail, bulk senders:**
  - Defined as about 5,000+ messages a day to personal Gmail accounts, counted per primary domain. The classification is **permanent**.
  - Requires SPF **and** DKIM, plus DMARC (p=none is acceptable) aligned with the From: domain.
  - One-click unsubscribe (RFC 8058 List-Unsubscribe headers) for marketing or promotional mail, honored within 48h.
  - **November 2025:** Gmail ramped up enforcement, including temporary and permanent rejections. Above 0.3% spam, a sender is ineligible for mitigation until 7 consecutive days below it.
  - Source: https://support.google.com/a/answer/14229414
- **Yahoo:** essentially the same. The 0.3% complaint rate is calculated on inbox-delivered mail; honor unsubscribes within 2 days. https://senders.yahooinc.com/best-practices/
- **Microsoft (Outlook.com, Hotmail, Live):** from May 5, 2025, senders of more than 5,000 a day need SPF, DKIM and DMARC (p=none or stricter) aligned. Non-compliant mail goes to junk, and later can be rejected outright with error 550 5.7.515. ([secondary] https://www.mailreach.co/blog/outlook-dmarc-requirements)
- **Operational practice:**
  - Instantly: new domains need 2–6 weeks of warmup. Start at 20–30 a day. Cap cold sends at about 30 per inbox per day, with about 100 a day as an absolute ceiling. Keep bounces under 2% (auto-pause at 5%). Send consistent volume, which yields 15–20% more replies than erratic volume.
  - Common practice: use a secondary look-alike domain for scaled cold outreach, plain text, no images, attachments or link-tracking in the first touch, and an opt-out line.
  - Legal: US CAN-SPAM requires an opt-out and a postal address; EU/UK B2B rules (GDPR "legitimate interest", PECR) differ. Have counsel verify.
- **For this product** (founder sends from their own Gmail or Workspace, no sending integration):
  - Founder volume (about 10–40 a day) is far below bulk thresholds. The real risks are **bounces from guessed addresses** and **spam complaints on the founder's primary domain**.
  - Rules to encode: daily cap (default 20 new cold sends a day per inbox); verify that SPF, DKIM and DMARC exist by reading the domain's public DNS; prefer LinkedIn or an intro when the email address is unverified; include an opt-out line; track complaints and unsubscribes manually.

### 3.5 LinkedIn norms
- **Limits:** about 100 invitations a week is the practical ceiling. It isn't published and varies with account age and acceptance rate. Personalized notes on free accounts are now heavily restricted; reports range from 3 to 10 a month at about 200 characters. Unverified, so design for a blank invite. ([secondary] https://www.salesforge.ai/blog/linkedin-connection-request-limit)
- **Sequence:**
  1. Engage genuinely with one of their posts.
  2. Connect, blank or with a 1-line note.
  3. After they accept, wait at least a day, then send a DM.
- **DM structure (Lavender "DM"):** context → insight or challenge → question. Under about 300 characters. No pitch, no calendar link.
- **Channel role:** use LinkedIn when email stalls (Belkins), for multi-threading, and for warm-ish founder-to-founder outreach. Founder posts about the problem build familiarity that lifts later reply rates (Linear and Hex built waitlists through content).

## 4. Signal-based selling

### 4.1 Evidence quality
Almost all signal-lift numbers are vendor-reported. The convergent, directional finding:
- A relevant signal plus interpretation lifts replies roughly 2–5x over generic outreach.
- 2–3 stacked signals on one account beat any single signal.
- A signal used only as flattery ("congrats on the raise") adds little. Autobound [vendor] claims 10–18% replies when referencing the specific use of capital vs. 3–5% for generic congratulations.

### 4.3 Turning a signal into an angle (the AI's core transformation)
1. **Signal:** what happened, with date and source URL.
2. **Interpretation:** what it means for this role.
3. **Pressure:** what new problem or risk it creates, in their language.
4. **Point of view:** the non-obvious insight. Why the obvious fix (status quo) fails.
5. **Proof:** a peer result or founder credential. Only if real.
6. **Ask:** an interest question.

Worked example:
- Signal: "Hiring 'Head of Revenue Operations' (posted 6 days ago); JD mentions Salesforce + usage billing in Stripe."
- Interpretation: they're formalizing revenue data ownership.
- Pressure: the new hire inherits mismatched usage and invoice data on day 1.
- POV: most teams try to fix this with a BI dashboard, which shows the mismatch but doesn't resolve it.
- Ask: "Is reconciliation part of what that hire will own?"

### 4.4 Signal hygiene rules
- **Fit first, signal second.** A signal at a non-ICP account is noise.
- **Freshness:** social signals under 7 days; hires and funding under 90 days; job changes under 90 days.
- **Stack signals.** Rank accounts by fit score × number of fresh signals.
- **Never be creepy.** No personal-life details, no "I saw you viewed…". Professional and public only.
- **Cite the source** in the founder-facing research card so the founder can verify before sending.

## 5. Discovery and deal progression

### 5.1 Founder deal stages, with exit criteria (see section 6 for metrics)
Target → Contacted → Engaged → Discovery booked → **Qualified** → Solution/Demo → Pilot (success criteria signed) → Proposal/Negotiation → Closed won / Closed lost (with reason) / Nurture.

### 5.2 Discovery call structure (30 minutes)
1. **Upfront contract (2 min):** purpose, time, agenda, and the explicit possibility that it isn't a fit. "By the end we'll both know whether a next step makes sense."
2. **Situation (5 min):** how the process works today, step by step, and who touches it.
3. **Pain (8 min):** follow Mom Test rules. Ask about past specifics, not hypotheticals. Last time it happened. What they've tried. ([secondary] https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)
4. **Impact (5 min):** quantify it (hours, dollars, revenue, risk), plus the emotional and personal impact.
5. **Critical event (3 min):** a date and the consequence of missing it.
6. **Decision (4 min):** people, process, criteria, paperwork, budget source.
7. **Optional tailored demo** (only if pain is confirmed) **or summary (2 min).**
8. **Next step booked on the calendar before hanging up (1 min).**

Gong data (https://www.gong.io/blog/winning-sales-conversations):
- Top performers talk 43% and listen 57% (2016 study). The 2025 average across all calls is 60:40 talking, so most people talk too much.
- Competitors mentioned early correlate with higher win rates; late mentions correlate with lower ones.
- Top reps cluster price discussion late in the call (minutes 40–49 of an hour).
- Buyers asking 3–4 pricing questions is the sweet spot.
- "Probably" about timing is a positive sign. "We need to figure out" is negative.
- Risk-reversal language (opt-outs, guarantees, SLAs) correlates with +32% win rate.
- The "11–14 questions" figure is widely cited but unverified.

Mom Test rule: a meeting succeeds only if it ends with a **commitment** (time, reputation or money) that **advances** the deal. Compliments are not data.

### 5.3 Frameworks to encode as call-note fields
- **SPICED (Winning by Design):** Situation, Pain, Impact (rational and emotional), Critical Event (a dated deadline with consequences; test: "What happens if you miss it?"), Decision (criteria, process, committee).
- **MEDDICC-lite for startups (7 fields):** Metrics (quantified impact), Economic buyer (named, met?), Decision criteria, Decision process plus paper process, Identified pain, Champion (tested?), Competition (including the status quo and build-in-house).
- **Gap Selling (Keenan):**
  - Current state: environment, problem, impact, root cause, emotion.
  - Future state: desired outcomes.
  - The gap: the measurable difference. Value is the size of the gap.
  - Use a Problem → Impact → Root Cause chart (https://www.gong.io/blog/gap-selling).
- **Qualification score** (0–2 per item, qualified if ≥8 of 12):

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain | Mild or vague | Clear but not urgent | Quantified and urgent |
| Current spend (money or time) | None | Some | Significant |
| Critical event | None | Soft target date | Hard date with consequences |
| Champion | None | Coach | Tested champion |
| Economic buyer | Unknown | Identified | Engaged |
| ICP fit | Poor | Partial | Strong |

### 5.4 Best discovery questions (bank for the AI to adapt)

**Situation**
- "Walk me through the last time you did [process], start to finish. Who was involved, and what tools?"
- "How often does this happen, and at what volume?"

**Pain**
- "What part of that do you hate most?" (Blomfield)
- "If you could wave a magic wand, what would disappear?" (Blomfield)
- "Tell me about the last time it went wrong. What happened next?"
- "What have you already tried? Why didn't it stick?" (Koomen, Mom Test)
- "Are you spending money or headcount on this today?" (Jen Abel's priority test)

**Impact**
- "What does that cost you per month in hours, dollars or deals?"
- "Who else feels this? How does it affect your boss's goals?"
- "If this were solved, what would you do with the time or money?"
- "What does leaving it unsolved cost by end of year?" (Koomen)

**Critical event**
- "Is there a date this has to be fixed by? What happens if you miss it?"
- "Why now rather than six months ago?"

**Decision and paperwork**
- "If you decided this was worth solving, what happens between yes and a signed contract?"
- "Who else weighs in: technical, security, legal, budget owner?"
- "How did the last tool purchase like this go, and how long did it take?"
- "Is budget already allocated, or would it need to be created? Whose budget?" (Koomen)
- "What would security need from us? Can we start that this week?"

**Champion test**
- "Would you be up for walking [economic buyer] through this with me?"
- "Could you share how you'd pitch this internally?"

**Pricing and willingness to pay** (Blomfield: ask early)
- "What would solving this be worth to you annually?"
- "What can you approve on your own without procurement?"

**Close the call**
- "Based on what you heard, does it make sense to [next step] on [date]?"
- "Who else should I talk to?" (First Round)

### 5.5 Objection handling
**Pattern:** acknowledge → clarify and isolate ("Is that the only thing stopping us?") → respond with evidence or a reframe → confirm → advance.

| Objection | Response approach |
|---|---|
| "You're too early / too small / no logos" | Risk reversal: paid pilot with success criteria, 30/60-day opt-out, founder-level SLA and direct line, security docs in progress with dates. Offer a design-partner role and its benefits (roadmap influence, pricing lock). Gong: risk-reversal language +32%. |
| "Just send me some info" | "Happy to. To send something useful rather than a generic deck: is [problem] relevant today, or should I check back in Q[x]?" |
| "No budget" | Ask whether the problem is costing money or time today. If yes, reframe against that cost and the champion's approval limit. If no, disqualify or nurture. |
| "We'll build it ourselves" | Quantify the true cost (engineers × months plus maintenance) and the opportunity cost, then offer a pilot faster than the build. |
| "We already use X" | Compliment X, then ask about the known gap (Lavender's competitor framework). Never badmouth. |
| "Not a priority right now" | Find the critical event. If none exists, nurture with a dated check-in, and ask "What would make this a priority?" |
| "Security / legal review will take months" | Start now: offer a security packet, a DPA template, and a minimal-data pilot (CSV, a 1% sample, one region) as Blomfield suggests. |
| "Price is too high" | Go back to quantified impact. Offer options (scope, term), not a bare discount. Discount only in exchange for something: a case study, multi-year, a reference, or signing by a date. |
| "Need to talk to my team" | "Of course. Who's on the team, what will they ask, and can I join or arm you with a one-pager?" Then book the follow-up. |

### 5.7 Post-call follow-up email (send same day)
1. **Subject:** "[Their company] + [yours]: recap and next steps"
2. **Thanks:** one line.
3. **"What I heard"** in their words: situation, top 1–2 pains, quantified impact, critical event.
4. **What we agreed:** the next step with date and time, and who's joining.
5. **Open questions or asks**, each with an owner and due date (e.g., a security contact, a sample data export).
6. **Answers** to anything promised on the call, and any link or resource.
7. **Close:** "Did I miss or misstate anything?" This invites correction and builds the champion's internal narrative.

Keep it under about 200 words. Bullets are fine here, unlike in cold email.

### 5.8 Proposal (1–2 pages, Koomen's "concise deck or one-pager")
1. Their situation and problem, in their words.
2. Quantified cost of the problem and the value case.
3. Success criteria and how they'll be measured.
4. Scope: what's included, what's not.
5. Timeline (link the MAP).
6. Price: 2–3 options, anchored on value, with the recommended option marked.
7. Commercial terms (opt-out window, pilot credit applied to the annual contract).
8. Proof (customers, credentials, security status).
9. Next step to sign, with a date.

Keep operational details (timelines, implementation) separate from the core legal agreement. Use simple standard paper.

### 5.9 Pilot and design-partner structures

| Structure | When | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| Free design partnership | Only for the first 1–2 customers when there's no product yet (Blomfield) | Hard cap of 60–90 days. Written conversion date and target price (heuristic). Founder weekly calls. |
| **Paid design partner** (Sierra / Bessemer) | Pre-launch or v0, larger ACV | 5–8 partners per cohort. About 10–20% of contract value up front. 2–6 months. Weekly 30-minute standups. Exec sponsor + technical + business contacts. Commitment to a real launch. Hard go-paid deadline. |
| **Paid pilot** (Blomfield) | Product works; buyer needs proof | 7–14 days. Fee within the champion's approval limit ($10–20K example). Success metric and ROI equation agreed in writing. Minimal integration (CSV or email). Check-ins every 2–3 days. ROI meeting booked at kickoff. Pilot fee credited to the annual contract. |
| **Annual contract with 30/60-day opt-out** (Blomfield) | Once you have 2+ references | Converts automatically if they don't opt out. Don't report opt-out-window revenue as ARR. |

**Success-criteria template:** "By [date], using [product] on [scope/sample], [customer] will achieve [metric] moving from [baseline] to [target], measured by [method/owner]. If met, [customer] will move to [plan] at [price] starting [date]."

Blomfield's example: an AI support tool claims to resolve 20% of tickets, which saves about $1M a year (a team of 100 down to 80). Price is $200K. The pilot covers 1,000 tickets with a pass range of 15–25%.

### 5.10 Pricing early
- **Value-based, not cost-plus.** YC's Kevin Hale argues value-based pricing lets founders charge considerably more (https://ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101). Undercharging is the most common mistake [secondary].
- **Get the inputs in discovery** (Koomen): cost of the problem today, headcount doing workarounds, available budget, spend on alternatives.
- **Heuristic:** price at about 10–20% of quantified annual value. Blomfield's example works out to 20%. This is a rule of thumb.
- **Anchor high:**
  - Enterprise: Jen Abel suggests starting at $75–150K. A tiny first deal makes expansion hard.
  - Under about $2K ACV needs self-serve [secondary].
  - Raise the quoted price with each new deal until you get real pushback (heuristic).
  - A win rate above about 40–50% may mean you're underpriced (Shah: https://siddharthsshah.substack.com/p/75-early-stage-sales).
- **Don't publish fixed prices too early.** Treat each quote as an experiment and log price quoted vs. outcome.
- **Discounts only in exchange for something:** case study, logo rights, multi-year term, prepayment, signing by a date.
- **Keep early packaging simple:** 1–3 options.

## 6. Pipeline stages, seed-stage metrics, weekly cadence

### 6.1 Stage definitions (exit criteria = what must be true to move on)

| Stage | Exit criteria |
|---|---|
| 0 Target | ICP fit score ≥ threshold, ≥1 fresh signal logged with source, persona identified |
| 1 Contacted | First touch sent (channel and angle logged) |
| 2 Engaged | Any human reply (positive, neutral or referral) |
| 3 Discovery booked | Meeting on calendar |
| 4 **Qualified opportunity** | SPICED pain + impact + critical event captured; champion identified; score ≥8/12; next step dated. **Only from here does it count as pipeline.** |
| 5 Solution / technical validation | Tailored demo done; technical approver engaged; security started |
| 6 Pilot | Success criteria signed; ROI meeting booked; fee agreed |
| 7 Proposal / Negotiation | Proposal sent; economic buyer engaged; MAP agreed |
| 8 Won / Lost / Nurture | Won: signed. Lost: reason code (no pain, no budget, timing, competitor, build, price, product gap, went dark). Nurture: re-engage date. |

### 6.2 Metrics that matter at seed
- **Inputs (weekly):**
  - Accounts researched.
  - New first touches by channel.
  - Warm intros requested and received.
  - Conversations held. Benchmarks: Sprig aimed for about 5 meetings a week; Okta for 15–18 new contacts a month.
- **Conversion rates:**
  - Reply rate (target ≥10% founder-targeted; 3.43% is the platform average).
  - Positive reply rate. Meetings booked as a share of contacted.
  - Discovery → qualified, qualified → pilot, pilot → paid (target ≥70% with signed success criteria; Sierra and Strella converted 100%).
  - New-logo win rate: 20–40% healthy, ~30% typical.
- **Velocity:**
  - Median days per stage and median sales cycle. Use the median, not the mean, because one large deal skews the mean.
  - Share of open deals with a dated next step (target 100%).
  - Deals stuck more than 14 days.
- **Outcomes:**
  - New ARR and number of logos.
  - Pipeline in dollars, qualified only.
  - Coverage target = 1 / win rate (25% win rate → 4x coverage).
  - New bookings growth: 7–12% a month is a good seed benchmark (Shah).
- **Learning:**
  - Conversations confirming each ICP and pain hypothesis.
  - Top objections, loss reasons, and the angles and signals with the best reply rates.
- **Post-sale:**
  - Time-to-first-value (Blomfield's north star).
  - Activation and usage vs. signed (one company signed $4M but implemented under $2M).
  - Retention: 90–110% good, 130%+ best.
- **Readiness to hire** (all three):
  - About 10 customers closed by the founder (Lemkin).
  - About 50 demos and a 20%+ win rate (Kazanjy, secondary).
  - A documented ICP, pitch, objection bank and stage criteria.

**Funnel math the product should compute, with an illustrative default:**
- Goal: 20 customers in 26 weeks at a 25% win rate → 80 qualified opportunities.
- At 50% discovery → qualified → 160 discovery calls, about 6 a week.
- If 40% come from warm, investor and community channels, about 96 must come from cold.
- At 3–5% meetings per contact, that's about 1,900–3,200 contacts, or about 75–125 a week (15–25 a weekday). That fits within founder deliverability caps.
- Lesson: blend warm and signal-based cold, and improve conversion before adding volume.

### 6.3 Weekly operating cadence for the founder
- **Daily (60–90 minutes, protected block):**
  - Work through the AI-prepared queue of 10–25 signal-backed drafts. The founder edits and sends.
  - Reply to every inbound response within hours.
  - 10–15 minutes of LinkedIn engagement.
  - 10-minute pre-call brief before each meeting. 10-minute post-call debrief after (SPICED fields, same-day follow-up email, MAP update).
- **Monday pipeline review (30–45 minutes):**
  - Last week's inputs vs. targets.
  - For each open deal: next step and date, champion status, economic-buyer status, critical event, days in stage, top risk.
  - Kill or nurture anything stalled more than 14 days with no dated next step.
  - Classify this month's closes as commit vs. best case.
  - Three priority deals plus one "whale."
- **Friday learning review (20–30 minutes):**
  - Reply rates by angle, signal, persona and subject.
  - Objections and loss reasons heard.
  - Quotes and language captured, fed back into the messaging tree.
  - ICP adjustment.
  - **One experiment for next week**, changing one variable at a time.
  - Customer health: time-to-first-value and usage for pilots and new customers.
- **Monthly:**
  - Revisit positioning and the ICP using the best-fit customers.
  - Pricing experiment review (quotes vs. outcomes).
  - 1–2 win/loss interviews.
  - Update the playbook document that becomes the first AE's onboarding.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR THE PRODUCT: 10 rules the AI Head of GTM must follow

1. **The founder sells; the AI does everything around the conversation.**
   - Never auto-send. Every message is a draft in the founder's voice, edited and sent by the founder.
   - The AI's job: research, prioritize, draft, prep, debrief, track and coach.
   - Push the founder to personally close the first 10–20 deals and build the documented playbook a future founding AE inherits.
   - Advise against hiring a VP or head of GTM before then.

2. **Run from a written, falsifiable ICP and update it after every conversation.**
   - Maintain the 4-part hypothesis (company group + role + obstacle + improvement), anti-ICP disqualifiers, and an evidence ledger.
   - One segment at a time.
   - Once real customers exist, rebuild the ICP from the best-fit ones: fastest to understand, fastest to buy, most value.

3. **Source outward by trust before going cold.** Every week, before generating cold lists, surface:
   - The founder's network: ex-colleagues, past users, and champions who changed jobs.
   - Investor portfolio founders.
   - Community threads.

4. **No signal, no send. Interpret every signal into a point of view.**
   - Every cold draft must cite a real, dated, public signal (with URL shown to the founder) and run Signal → Interpretation → Pressure → POV → Proof → Ask.
   - Reject decorative personalization: if deleting line 1 leaves a generic email, rewrite it.
   - Stack signals to prioritize accounts.

5. **Write like a respected peer, not a sequencer.**
   - 40–80 words (hard cap 100), a 1–4 word specific subject, one problem, one interest-based question.
   - No calendar link or attachment on first touch. No buzzwords ("AI-powered platform"). "You" outnumbers "I".
   - 3–5 touches, each with a new angle; then switch channel; recycle in 60–90 days.
   - Never invent customers, metrics or social proof. With no proof, use founder credibility, insight, or a design-partner invitation.

6. **Guard deliverability and reputation as hard constraints.**
   - Check the founder domain's SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
   - Cap new cold sends at about 20–30 per inbox per day.
   - Flag unverified or guessed addresses.
   - Include an opt-out line and log opt-outs.
   - Keep complaints effectively at zero (Google and Yahoo: under 0.1% target, 0.3% hard line).
   - Prefer LinkedIn or a warm path when email confidence is low.

7. **Treat every call as diagnosis, and fail any meeting without a dated next step.**
   - Generate a pre-call brief with hypotheses and SPICED/MEDDICC-lite questions.
   - After the call, extract the fields, score qualification (0–12), and draft the same-day recap in the customer's words.
   - Coach the founder to listen more than talk, keep "about us" under 2 minutes, raise competitors early, and never leave without a calendared commitment.

8. **Charge from day one and make success criteria explicit.**
   - Default structures: paid pilots (7–14 days, within the champion's approval limit, ROI meeting pre-booked, fee credited), or paid design partnerships (10–20% up front, written conversion date and price).
   - Then annual contracts with an opt-out.
   - Price on quantified value (about 10–20% of annual impact), anchor high, discount only in exchange for something.
   - Alert when any engagement has been unpaid for more than 30 days.

9. **Map the committee, multi-thread, and run a mutual action plan for every qualified deal.**
   - Identify champion vs. coach, economic buyer, technical, security and legal.
   - Start security and paperwork the week the deal qualifies.
   - Keep the MAP's owners and dates current. Draft the champion's internal one-pager for them.

10. **Run the weekly operating rhythm and let the numbers decide.**
    - Monday pipeline review and Friday learning review. Only qualified deals count as pipeline. Coverage = 1 / win rate.
    - Kill or nurture deals with no next step for 14 days.
    - Report inputs → conversions → outcomes → learnings, with one experiment per week.
    - Diagnose failure at the earliest broken stage:
      - Low replies: fix ICP or angle, not volume.
      - Low discovery → qualified: fix targeting.
      - Low pilot → paid: fix success criteria and time-to-value.
      - A win rate that's too high: raise prices.
